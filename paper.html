Outcome-dependent post-selection is sufficient to reproduce the reported Bell violation regime from a purely classical source when detection efficiency collapses.

  • A classical hidden-variable model stays at S = 2.001 without selection and rises to S = 3.716 with biased acceptance.
  • At Wang et al.’s reported efficiency of ~10−18, the experiment sits deep inside the detection-loophole-vulnerable regime.
  • A convincing low-efficiency Bell claim therefore requires explicit diagnostics, not just a post-selected CHSH value above 2.

Abstract

A recent Science Advances paper claims Bell-inequality violation without entanglement. We argue that the reported result — a CHSH value of S = 2.275 ± 0.057 at an effective detection efficiency of roughly 10−18 — does not support that conclusion on its own. At efficiencies this low, outcome-dependent selection can inflate CHSH statistics regardless of whether the underlying source is quantum, classical, or separable. We show this directly with a deterministic local hidden-variable simulation: without post-selection the model yields S = 2.001 (95% CI [1.998, 2.004]) at 100% acceptance, while the same source with biased acceptance yields S = 3.716 (95% CI [3.714, 3.717]) at 70.0% acceptance. The implication is narrow but decisive: a post-selected CHSH value above 2 is not, by itself, evidence of nonlocality in a regime where classical selection artifacts remain available. We conclude with a diagnostic protocol for future low-efficiency experiments.

Wang et al. report S = 2.275 ± 0.057 from a multiphoton interference experiment and interpret that result as Bell-inequality violation without entanglement [12]. The central issue in this paper is not whether their optical setup is impressive. It is whether a post-selected CHSH value in an ultra-low-efficiency regime can rule out local classical explanations.

This paper makes one narrow claim: the reported statistic is not self-authenticating evidence of nonlocality when selection effects remain available.

The Detection Loophole

The inference from S > 2 to nonlocality depends on fair sampling. When detection efficiency drops, the surviving sample can cease to represent the emitted ensemble. Pearle and Eberhard showed that low-efficiency Bell experiments are vulnerable to local models that exploit selection effects [3] [7].

Results

Baseline vs. Post-Selected Artifact

Without post-selection, the classical model yields S = 2.001 (95% CI [1.998, 2.004]) at 100% acceptance, as expected for a local hidden-variable source. With biased retention, the same source yields S = 3.716 (95% CI [3.714, 3.717]) at 70.0% acceptance. The important point is not just that the inflated statistic exceeds 2. It exceeds the Tsirelson bound as well.

Why Wang et al. Remain Non-Diagnostic Here

Wang et al. report a value below our classical artifact, but that is not the key comparison. The decisive point is simpler: the rebuttal model proves that local classical data can occupy the reported regime once selection effects are admitted. At η \approx 10^{-18}, the efficiency-adjusted local ceiling is so large that S = 2.275 is plainly not exclusionary evidence against local models.

The result need not be “more extreme than Wang” to matter. It only has to demonstrate that local post-selection can generate the class of evidence being interpreted as nonlocal.

Discussion

What Would Make a Bell Claim Convincing?

The standard was set by the loophole-free Bell tests of 2015, which combined sufficiently high efficiency with locality protections and careful diagnostics [9] [10] [11]. Those experiments did not rest their case on a post-selected CHSH number alone.

Table 3. Minimum diagnostic package for low-efficiency Bell claims.
Diagnostic Why it matters
Raw correlations before selection Shows whether the effect exists before the filtering step.
Acceptance rate by setting pair Tests whether retention depends on measurement context.
No-signaling checks on selected and unselected data Detects selection-induced inconsistencies across marginals.
Independence tests for acceptance vs. settings Checks whether the filter is entangled with the measurement choices.
Sensitivity analysis for selection bias Quantifies how much artifact could be created by retention asymmetry.

Scope, Objections, and Limits

This paper does not prove that Wang et al.’s apparatus is classical. It proves that the reported statistic does not exclude classical post-selection artifacts. A defender of the original claim could still recover it by showing that their filtering cannot depend on outcomes in a way that biases the estimator, or by providing diagnostics that eliminate the relevant local alternatives.

That is why the burden here is evidentiary, not rhetorical. Once efficiency falls this far below the fair-sampling threshold, the experiment must report enough structure to discriminate genuine nonlocality from a selection artifact. Without that structure, a CHSH value above 2 should be treated as suggestive at most, not decisive.

The strongest skeptical objection is “perhaps the apparatus is still quantum.” That objection is compatible with this paper. The point is that the reported evidence does not force the nonlocal interpretation.

Conclusion

The core finding is straightforward. A local hidden-variable model with outcome-dependent post-selection reproduces the qualitative regime of the reported Bell claim and surpasses the Tsirelson bound, all without entanglement. That means the Wang et al. result, taken as a post-selected CHSH statistic at ultra-low efficiency, does not by itself establish quantum nonlocality.

The experiment remains technically impressive. But technical impressiveness is not the same thing as a loophole-free inference. To make the claim persuasive, future iterations need transparent diagnostic reporting that rules out precisely the selection mechanism demonstrated here.
