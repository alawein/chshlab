Bell Violations Without Entanglement?
A Reproducible Rebuttal of Wang et al. (2025)

Meshal Alawein · UC Berkeley · contact@meshal.ai

A recent paper in Science Advances claims to demonstrate Bell-inequality violation without entanglement. We argue that the reported result — a CHSH value of S = 2.275 ± 0.057 obtained at a detection efficiency of roughly 10−18 — does not support this conclusion. When acceptance rates drop this low, outcome-dependent selection can inflate CHSH statistics regardless of whether the underlying source is quantum, classical, or separable. We demonstrate this with explicit simulations: a purely classical hidden-variable model, subjected to similar post-selection, yields S = 3.716 (95% CI: [3.714, 3.717]), well above both the classical bound (S ≤ 2) and the Tsirelson bound (S ≤ 2√2 ≈ 2.83). We provide a straightforward diagnostic protocol for future experiments.

The inference from S > 2 to nonlocality requires a critical assumption: that the detected sample fairly represents the full ensemble. When detection efficiency η falls below a critical threshold ηc ≈ 82.8%, this assumption fails (Pearle, 1970; Eberhard, 1993).

Wang et al. (2025) reported S = 2.275 ± 0.057 from a multiphoton interference experiment with an effective detection efficiency of approximately 10−18. We demonstrate that this result is fully consistent with a classical local hidden-variable model subjected to outcome-dependent post-selection.

Methods

Simulation Framework

We construct a classical hidden-variable model with a single parameter λ drawn uniformly from [0, 2π). Alice’s outcome is A(a, λ) = sign(cos(a − λ)) and Bob’s is B(b, λ) = sign(cos(b − λ)). This model is explicitly local and deterministic.

Post-Selection Model

We introduce outcome-dependent selection: events where A · B = +1 are kept with probability phi, while events where A · B = −1 are kept with probability plo. When phi > plo, the filter preferentially retains correlated outcomes.

For our primary simulation: phi = 0.9, plo = 0.1, with N = 106 trials per setting pair.

Results

Classical Baseline

Without post-selection (phi = plo = 1.0), our classical model produces S = 2.001 (95% CI: [1.998, 2.004]) at 100% acceptance rate. This is consistent with the CHSH bound, confirming the model’s classical nature.

Post-Selected Results

With outcome-dependent selection (phi = 0.9, plo = 0.1), the same classical source yields S = 3.716 (95% CI: [3.714, 3.717]) at 70.0% acceptance rate. This exceeds both the classical bound (S = 2) and the quantum Tsirelson bound (S = 2√2 ≈ 2.828).

Comparison with Wang et al.

Wang et al. reported S = 2.275 ± 0.057 at an effective detection efficiency of ~10−18. At this efficiency, the LHV maximum is astronomically large: SLHV,max = 4/10−18 − 2 ≈ 4 × 1018. Their reported value falls well within the regime where classical models can trivially reproduce the result.

Discussion

What Makes a Bell Test Convincing?

The loophole-free Bell tests of 2015 (Hensen et al.; Giustina et al.; Shalm et al.) achieved detection efficiencies above the critical threshold while simultaneously closing the locality loophole. These experiments set the standard for Bell claims.

A Diagnostic Protocol

For experiments operating below ηc, we recommend reporting: (1) raw correlations before selection; (2) acceptance rates per setting pair; (3) no-signaling tests on both selected and unselected data; (4) independence tests between acceptance and measurement settings; (5) sensitivity analysis for selection bias.

Conclusion

We have demonstrated that outcome-dependent post-selection can produce CHSH values well above the Tsirelson bound from purely classical data. The Wang et al. result — obtained at detection efficiency ~10−18 — does not constitute evidence for quantum nonlocality. The experiment represents impressive optical engineering, but the statistical claim requires either closing the detection loophole or providing explicit diagnostics ruling out selection artifacts.
